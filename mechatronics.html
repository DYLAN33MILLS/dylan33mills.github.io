The challenge

Find the color.
Navigate the arena.

Our six-person team built a robot for a balloon-popping competition. The arena combined colored balloons, walls, and a white-tape boundary, requiring perception and movement to work together.

The architecture split the problem into two subsystems: a local vision-tracking turret and a mobile platform designed to detect obstacles, stay within the boundary, and approach the assigned balloon color.

My focus: turret controls & embedded integration.

  • Developed C++ tracking controls using a Pixy2 camera and Arduino.
  • Iterated the turret actuation from servo to stepper to DC motor.
  • Tuned feedback control to balance tracking response, overshoot, and oscillation.
  • Integrated sensing, motor control, and the rotating mechanical assembly.
The chassis, navigation software, mechanism, and final report were shared team deliverables. Platform examples below show the wider system my work integrated with.
System architecture

Two systems.
One physical robot.

Each subsystem handled its own sensing and actuation. The turret tracked locally while the platform managed the robot’s movement through the arena.

Turret subsystem

Local tracking loop

  1. Pixy2 cameraRecognizes trained balloon colors
  2. Arduino UnoProcesses vision input and feedback control
  3. Rotating turret assemblyDC motor provides continuous rotation

Keeping the turret’s controller and power on the rotating assembly helped avoid cable winding during rotation.

Mobile platform

Perception and movement

  1. Camera + ultrasonic + IRColor, obstacle, and boundary inputs
  2. Raspberry Pi → Arduino UnoPerception and navigation → motor commands
  3. Four-wheel mecanum driveForward, reverse, strafe, and rotation

The Pi–Arduino interface used I²C during development, then USB serial as a late fallback after a communication failure.

Iteration & control

The right motion
took more than one motor.

The turret progressed through three actuator approaches as the team balanced available hardware, controllability, and the need for continuous rotation.

  1. 01 / SERVO

    Establish the loop.

    Early camera and servo experiments connected visual detection to physical motion and provided a starting point for controller development.

  2. 02 / STEPPER

    Explore position control.

    A stepper prototype used AccelStepper, image-based feedback, and a limit-switch input while the turret design evolved.

  3. 03 / DC MOTOR

    Enable continuous rotation.

    The final report documents a DC-driven turret. Iterative tuning focused on tracking response while reducing overshoot and oscillation.

Testing beyond the first response.

Turret testing checked whether tracking remained consistent through different operating states and whether the selected balloon color was retained. The report records corrections for both behaviors.

Make the interfaces work.

Separating the subsystems simplified local development. Integration still depended on practical details: wiring, available power, component mounting, and communication timing.

A readable movement interface

Python · movement_commands.py
Named commands keep navigation decisions separate from the serial transport to the drive controller.

def send_command(command):
    arduino.write(command.encode())

def move_forward():
    send_command('MOVE_FORWARD')

def move_left():
    send_command('MOVE_LEFT')

def rotate_right():
    send_command('ROTATE_RIGHT')

Integration & results

The result.
And the lessons that mattered.

Subsystem testing and full-robot operation produced different outcomes. The final report’s competition and future-work sections provide the clearest account of what was demonstrated.

SUBSYSTEM RESULT

Tracking developed through testing.

The report describes a functional vision-tracking turret after motor tuning and corrections to color selection and state handling. Tracking performance was assessed qualitatively during prototype testing.

SYSTEM LIMITATION

Communication constrained navigation.

An I²C failure prompted a switch to USB serial shortly before competition. Command timing, buffering, and missing acknowledgments disrupted platform behavior. The team used a predetermined movement sequence as a fallback; reliable full autonomous navigation remained unresolved.

Integration has to be tested as a system. Working sensors and motors are only part of the result; communication timing and recovery behavior determine how consistently they work together.

The next engineering step

The report proposes consolidating time-sensitive sensor and motor control on one microcontroller, with the Pi supplying camera information, to reduce the platform’s dependence on repeated cross-controller commands.

Further development

Better sensor coverage, more secure wiring, a slimmer package, and encoder feedback were identified as future improvements. Optical-flow and odometry files document experiments, rather than proven final capabilities.
